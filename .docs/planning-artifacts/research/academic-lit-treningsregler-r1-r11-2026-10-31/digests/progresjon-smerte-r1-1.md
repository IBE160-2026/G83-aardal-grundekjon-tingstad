## How to read the read-depth labels

Most sources were seen only through search-result excerpts, which is weaker than reading an abstract.

- **full text (partial)**: the page was fetched, but answers came through an extraction model and only part of the page was covered.
- **abstract**: the abstract page was fetched.
- **snippet**: search-result excerpt or summary only; the source itself was NOT opened. Verify before citing as final.
- **Inference**: my own reasoning from retrieved sources, not a sourced finding.
- **Unverified belief**: not evidenced in this run.

Only three pages returned readable content: Hickmott 2022, Bickel 2011 and Helsedirektoratet ch. 6.9. The MDCG PDF was fetched but could not be read. Every claim marked snippet needs checking.

## Part A: Progression

### A1. Load increase

**Claim 1.** ACSM 2009 recommends a 2-10% load increase when the person can perform the current workload for 1-2 repetitions over the desired number.
- Source: Ratamess et al., ACSM Position Stand, Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/
- Publisher: Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708 (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2009
- Confidence: high that this is what it says; medium as a current default (see claim 2). Class: guideline. Read depth: snippet.

**Claim 2.** ACSM 2009 has been updated. The 2026 position stand ("Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews") draws on 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants. It concludes that healthy adults should perform progressive resistance training, and that the largest gain comes from going from no training to any training. Failure training, equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood flow restriction and periodization did not consistently affect outcomes.
- Sources: APTA listing https://apta.org/patient-care/evidence-based-practice-resources/cpgs/american-college-of-sports-medicine-position-stand-resistance-training-prescription-for-muscle-function-hypertrophy-and-physical-performance-in-healthy-adults-an-overview-of-reviews ; press release https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years ; ACSM infographic https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf
- Publisher: ACSM / MSSE. pub_date: 2026 (exact date not captured; infographic path indicates March 2026)
- Confidence: medium. Class: guideline. Read depth: snippet; the full text and the infographic were not opened. The press release is promotional.
- **Key gap:** I could NOT establish whether the 2026 stand keeps, changes or drops the 2-10% rule. Do not cite 2-10% as current ACSM guidance until this is checked.

**Claim 3 (secondary, weak).** A commentary says the 2026 stand supports about 10 sets per muscle group per week to optimize hypertrophy, and a summary gives about 80% 1RM for 2-3 sets per exercise for strength.
- Source: search result set above, including NFPT commentary https://nfpt.com/beyond-the-guidelines-what-the-2026-acsm-position-stand-gets-right-and-what-it-changes-for-personal-trainers/ (the excerpts did not clearly map each figure to one URL)
- pub_date: 2026. Confidence: low. Class: guideline (secondary report). Read depth: snippet.

**Inference: fixed 2.5 kg.** Applied to the 2-10% band, 2.5 kg stays inside it only for working loads of about 25-125 kg (2.5/0.10 and 2.5/0.02). At 10 kg it is a 25% jump, and at 5 kg it is 50%. So a fixed 2.5 kg step is inconsistent with ACSM 2009 for small exercises and light loads, which are common for beginners. A percentage rule rounded to available equipment, with reps as the fallback when the jump is too large, fits the source better. No source tested this; it is arithmetic plus judgment.

**Finding: "2-for-2 rule".** I retrieved no primary source for it. The nearest retrieved support is the ACSM 2009 "1-2 repetitions over" wording. Its usual attribution is an unverified belief, and no dedicated search was run for it.

**Finding: fixed increment.** No retrieved source compares fixed-kilogram steps with percentage steps, or addresses them for small exercises or beginners.

### A2. Load vs reps

**Claim 4.** In an 8-week RCT of 43 participants with at least 1 year of lower-body training (4 sets of 4 lower-body exercises, twice weekly), both load and repetition progression were viable. Rectus femoris growth modestly favoured reps. Dynamic strength slightly favoured load (2.0 kg, interval -2.4 to 7.8), with differences of questionable practical significance.
- Source: Plotkin et al., Progressive overload without progressing load? https://peerj.com/articles/14142
- Publisher: PeerJ (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2022
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet (abstract-level content).
- Limits: "viable" is not a demonstrated equivalence. The study is small, short, lower-body only and in trained people, and the interval is wide. A commentary notes a high starting rep range (8-12) and training to failure: https://lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/increasing-weight-or-increasing-reps-can-both-make-you-stronger/ . Application to beginners or upper-body and small exercises is an extrapolation.

### A3. Perceived effort

**Claim 5.** Load autoregulation (RPE/RIR and velocity) did not differ significantly from standardized percentage loading for 1RM (MD 2.07 kg, CI -0.32 to 4.46, p = 0.09; 6 studies, 133 participants). The RPE subgroup showed a near-significant small effect (MD 3.15 kg, p = 0.06, SMD 0.30). Participants were mostly resistance-trained young men. All studies had some risk of bias, and a fixed-effects model was used.
- Source: Hickmott et al., systematic review and meta-analysis. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534
- Publisher: Sports Medicine - Open (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2022
- Confidence: medium (low for beginners or recreational adults). Class: empirical. Read depth: full text (partial, first ~100k of ~196k characters).
- Reading: autoregulation looks about as good as fixed loading, not shown to be better. This defends RPE on non-inferiority-style grounds only, and the study was not designed as a formal non-inferiority test (my inference).

**Claim 6.** Hickmott describes intra-set RPE as inaccurate and subjective, improving with practice and closer to failure. It does not call RPE or RIR validated.
- Source and details as claim 5. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: full text (partial).

**Claim 7.** RIR accuracy depends on proximity to failure and load intensity.
- Source: Russo et al., systematic review. https://rdpc.uevora.pt/handle/10174/39438
- pub_date: 2025. Journal not captured. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**Claim 8.** The Borg CR10 RPE scale did not appear reliable in resistance training, according to this review of autoregulation methods.
- Source: Larsen, Kristiansen, van den Tillaar. https://peerj.com/articles/10663
- Publisher: PeerJ (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2021
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**Claim 9.** Autoregulation has been used since the 1940s but seldom studied systematically, and definitions are inconsistent.
- Source: Larsen et al., Autoregulation in Resistance Training: Addressing the Inconsistencies. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7575491/
- Publisher: Sports Medicine (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2020
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (review). Read depth: snippet.

**Claim 10.** A network meta-analysis ranked APRE first (SUCRA 97.1%), then velocity-based (57.1%), RPE (29.9%) and percentage-based training (15.9%) for maximal strength.
- Source: Huang et al. Record: https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi.dedup.....173ee6edddd6beccbd6c240cadbf44a7
- pub_date and journal not captured. Confidence: low (SUCRA ranks are not effect sizes; library record only). Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**Finding: easy/ok/hard.** I found no validation of a simple three-point easy/ok/hard scale. No dedicated search was run, so this is "not found", not "does not exist". Session RPE evidence (Foster and others) was NOT retrieved in this run. Treat a three-point scale as an unvalidated product heuristic for now.

### A4. Reducing volume

**Claim 11.** After 16 weeks of training three times weekly, 70 adults were randomized to 32 weeks of detraining or maintenance at 1/3 or 1/9 of the original dose. Both doses preserved hypertrophy in the young (20-35) but not the old (60-75). Strength gains were largely retained even through detraining, with a slight decline at the final time point.
- Source: Bickel, Cross, Bamman. Exercise dosing to retain resistance training adaptations in young and older adults. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/
- Publisher: Med Sci Sports Exerc 43(7):1177-87 (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2011
- Confidence: high for this finding; medium for applying it here. Class: empirical. Read depth: abstract.

**Claim 12.** Androulakis-Korakakis et al. address the minimum dose to INCREASE 1RM in trained men/powerlifters, not to maintain it. The powerlifter paper's advice is autoregulated sets at RPE 7.5-9.5 for 6-12 weeks.
- Sources: https://www.ufrgs.br/sees-initiative/pmid31797219/ (listed as Sports Med. 2019; the question cites 2020, and the online vs print date was not confirmed) ; preprint version https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/preprint/The-minimum-effective-training-dose-required/99533806302621 ; powerlifter paper record https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-25453521684f492caa7b44bbaef2d546
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.
- Correction for the planner: do not cite this as a maintenance source. It is a progression-dose source.

**Claim 13.** In trained adults, a one-week deload at the midpoint of a 9-week programme did not affect lower-body hypertrophy, power or local muscular endurance, but strength gains were modestly smaller than with continuous training.
- Source: Coleman et al. https://peerj.com/articles/16777
- Publisher: PeerJ (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2024
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet. The excerpt did not state the deload's content; check whether it was reduced training or full cessation before using it to set deload design.

**Claim 14.** A within-person trial in untrained young men found no statistical disadvantage of a planned deload for muscle thickness or 10RM.
- Source: Pancar et al., Scientific Reports, seen only through a secondary summary set, e.g. https://sci-sport.com/en/active-deload-in-strength-training-does-temporarily-reducing-volume-hinder-progress-270/
- pub_date: 2026. Confidence: low until the primary paper is opened. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet (secondary).

**Claim 15.** In velocity-based volume autoregulation, velocity loss above 25% gave more hypertrophy than 25% or less (MD 0.61 cm2, p = 0.03; 3 studies), while 25% or less was better for 1RM (MD 2.32 kg, p = 0.02), partly driven by a confounded study. The authors note volume was not equated.
- Source: Hickmott, as claim 5. Confidence: low-medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: full text (partial).
- Reading: this is the only pooled "volume autoregulation" evidence retrieved. It concerns within-set velocity loss, NOT energy, readiness or sleep. It must not be presented as support for a readiness rule.

**Inference: dropping one set.**
- Direct evidence: none found for dropping one set on low energy, readiness or sleep.
- Indirect defence: if 32 weeks at 1/3 or 1/9 of the dose preserved adaptations in young adults (claim 11), and a one-week deload had little cost beyond a modest strength effect (claim 13), one occasional dropped set is very unlikely to matter. That makes the rule defensible as low-cost, not as evidence-based for recovery or benefit.
- Limits: Bickel's older group lost hypertrophy, so repeated reductions in older users are less safe to assume. A rule firing on most sessions becomes a chronic dose reduction, and the 2026 stand's volume message (claim 3, low confidence) is relevant to that.

**Finding: sleep and readiness.** No retrieved trial tests reducing volume based on low sleep, energy or readiness. A "poor sleep reduces strength about 12%" figure appeared only in a secondary web article (https://keedia-website.pages.dev/fitness/deload-protocols-research-evidence/ or a neighbouring result) and could not be verified. Do not use it.

## Part B: Pain and safety

### B5. Screening, red flags, training with pain

**Claim 16.** The 2015 ACSM screening model rests on current physical activity, signs/symptoms and/or known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and desired intensity. Risk-factor profiling was removed because it led to excessive physician referrals and could be a barrier to exercise.
- Source: Riebe et al., Updating ACSM's Recommendations for Exercise Preparticipation Health Screening. https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/180
- Publisher: Med Sci Sports Exerc 47(11):2473-2479 (peer-reviewed). pub_date: 2015
- Confidence: medium-high. Class: guideline. Read depth: snippet; the original paper's algorithm chart was not seen. Whether a later ACSM edition changed it was not checked.

**Claim 17.** Applied to NHANES 2001-2004, the algorithm would refer 54.2% of adults before any exercise and 2.6% only before vigorous exercise, 41% fewer referrals than the previous version. Validation for correctly identifying those at risk is still needed.
- Source: Whitfield, Riebe, Magal, Liguori. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860 (URL-to-paper mapping inferred from the result set; verify). Secondary report: https://www.acefitness.org/education-and-resources/professional/expert-articles/6921/new-preparticipation-screening-guidelines-what-health-and-fitness-pros-need-to-know/
- pub_date not captured. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**Inference.** The model as summarized addresses cardiovascular, metabolic and renal risk. It does not appear to cover musculoskeletal pain, so it cannot be cited as the basis for a pain rule. The full paper should confirm this.

**Claim 18 (weak sources only).** A PAR-Q+ "yes" leads to follow-up questions rather than automatic clearance or barring. Classic PAR-Q items ask about chest pain during activity and at rest. Stop-exercise signs listed in non-authoritative guides: chest pressure or pain, pain in the neck, jaw, arm or shoulder, dizziness or feeling sick, cold sweat, racing or irregular heartbeat, collapse, severe breathlessness.
- Sources: https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/crossfit-l3/screening-assessment/screening-health-medical ; https://assets.aarp.org/www.aarp.org_/articles/learn/sidebars/4-parq.htm ; https://www.portlandbiblecollege.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Basic_Exercise_Guidelines_and_Tips.pdf
- Confidence: low. Class: guideline (secondary reproductions). Read depth: snippet.
- Red flag: an exam-prep site and a college handout are not acceptable sources for a safety rule. The content fits what I would expect, but the official CSEP PAR-Q+ and a cardiology or emergency authority must be retrieved before this list goes into the product.

**Claim 19.** In a 2007 RCT in Achilles tendinopathy, continued loading with pain capped below 5 on a 0-10 VAS had no detrimental effect on outcomes compared with pain-free activity modification.
- Source: Silbernagel et al., via summary https://physicaltherapyfirst.com/blog/continued-sports-activity-using-a-pain-monitoring-model-during-rehabilitation-in-patients-with-achilles-tendinopathy/
- pub_date of original: 2007. Confidence: medium-low (primary not opened). Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet (secondary).
- Limits: this is a diagnosed patient population in rehabilitation, not undiagnosed app users. The transfer is an inference.

**Claim 20.** An Achilles trial protocol keeps pain within a "Progress" zone on a 1-10 scale and negotiates reduced load, speed or repetitions if pain is too high.
- Source: TEAM 1 trial, QMUL. https://tendon.qmul.ac.uk/patients/treatments/team1
- pub_date not captured. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (protocol). Read depth: snippet.

**Claim 21.** Evidence for exercise in hamstring, insertional Achilles and upper-limb tendinopathy is limited. A systematic review of load-progression criteria (Escriche-Escuder et al., 2020) concluded pain monitoring might be important but may not be the most suitable criterion for load progression.
- Sources: case report https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4OgqBkq4 ; the Escriche-Escuder review was seen only via excerpt in the result set, with no direct URL confirmed.
- Confidence: low-medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**Red flag.** The commonly repeated "pain above 5/10 or lasting over 24 hours" rule retrieved here came from a non-peer-reviewed trainer article (https://www.nasm.org/resource-center/blog/training/tendon-health-and-load-managament-what-trainers-get-wrong). Do not cite it as evidence.

**Inference: is "no plan change, contact a professional" right?** No retrieved source tests this rule for apps. My reasoning, on the evidence above:
- **Under-cautious for red flags.** "Contact a professional" lacks urgency for chest symptoms, collapse or similar (claim 18, weak sources). Such a message should say to stop and seek urgent care.
- **Not actually conservative for pain.** "No plan change" means the plan continues unchanged, including possible load increases, while the user is in pain. Automatic progression should at least pause for the affected exercise.
- **Probably over-cautious for ordinary, mild training pain.** Claims 16-17 show referral-heavy screening was treated as a barrier, and claim 19 suggests some pain-limited loading is not harmful in one diagnosed condition. Evidence for extending this to undiagnosed users is absent.
- **Candidate design (unverified, needs clinical and legal review):** (1) red-flag symptoms: stop and urgent care; (2) other pain or injury mention: no automatic progression on the affected exercise, no diagnosis, and a clear suggestion to see a professional, with the user choosing what to do; (3) do not build a pain-score-driven load algorithm for undiagnosed users, because the evidence comes from diagnosed patients and it may move the product toward a medical purpose (see claim 25).

**Claim 29.** CJEU Grand Chamber, C-21/23 Lindenapotheke (4 October 2024): order data for pharmacy-only medicines is health data even without a prescription. It is sufficient that health status can be inferred by mental combination or deduction, and the accuracy of the information and the controller's intent are immaterial.
- Sources: press release https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2024-10/cp240159en.pdf ; commentary https://www.europeanlawblog.eu/pub/y0sz1qv4 ; https://www.thomashelbing.com/en/wissen/dsgvo-hub/rechtsprechung/1.4.31-eugh-lindenapotheke
- Publisher: CJEU and commentators. pub_date: 2024. Confidence: medium-high. Class: regulatory (case law). Read depth: snippet; judgment not opened.

**Inference: training and pain data as health data.**
- Pain or injury statements linked to an identifiable user very likely qualify, since they directly reveal health status (strong inference from claim 29).
- Sets, reps and loads alone are less clear. No retrieved authority addresses them. But the inference test is broad, and these logs combined with pain, sleep or energy entries raise the likelihood.
- Conservative design assumption: treat the combined user record as Article 9 data.
- GDPR art. 4(15) and recital 35 text was not retrieved in this run. Legal review is needed. Applicability in Norway through the EEA is an unverified belief here.
