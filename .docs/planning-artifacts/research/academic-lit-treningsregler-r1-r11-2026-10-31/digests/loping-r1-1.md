# Digest: running planner defaults (loping-r1-1)

Accessed for all sources: 2026-10-31. Budget used: 20 tool calls before this write (2 rounds), so several leads remain unread.

**Read depth labels**
- **abstract**: I fetched the page, and it showed the abstract or record only.
- **snippet**: I saw only a search-result summary. The page was not fetched. Treat the figures as provisional until checked.
- No paper was read in full text in this run. The only full page read was an editorial (claim 3.3).

All listed papers appear to be peer-reviewed journal articles. No preprints were used. Where the venue or date was not visible, the claim says "not confirmed".

## Q1. The 10% rule and progression metrics

**1.1 A graded programme based on the 10% rule did not reduce injuries in novices, compared with a standard programme.** 20.8% vs 20.3% injured, P = .90, n = 532, preparing for a 4-mile event; 13-week graded vs 8-week standard programme.
- Source: Buist et al., "No effect of a graded training program on the number of running-related injuries in novice runners: a randomized controlled trial", https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/no-effect-of-a-graded-training-program-on-the-number-of-running-r/
- Publisher: Am J Sports Med 36(1):33-39. pub_date: January 2008
- Confidence: high for this population and short event; low for generalising to half-marathon runners
- Class: empirical (RCT). Read depth: abstract
- Unverified: a secondary summary says the standard arm's weekly increases reached up to 53%. I did not confirm this in the paper.

**1.2 In novices, weekly-distance progression was not associated with overall injury. There was a non-significant signal for distance-related injuries above 30%.** Over a two-week period, more than 30% vs under 10%: HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96-2.66, P = .07). n = 874, 202 injured, GPS, one-year exploratory cohort. The authors suggest staying under 30% over two weeks and call for RCTs.
- Source: Nielsen et al., "Excessive progression in weekly running distance and risk of running-related injuries: an association which varies according to type of injury", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25155475/ (the PMID was read from a PubMed search page, which displayed this record)
- Publisher: JOSPT 44(10):739-47. pub_date: October 2014
- Confidence: medium (exploratory, CI crosses 1)
- Class: empirical. Read depth: abstract

**1.3 A single-session spike is associated with injury, while the week-to-week ratio is not.** Reference is regression or an increase up to 10% over the longest run in the previous 30 days.
- Over 10-30%: HRR 1.64 (1.31-2.05)
- Over 30-100%: HRR 1.52 (1.16-2.00)
- Over 100%: HRR 2.28 (1.50-3.48)
- ACWR showed a negative dose-response. The week-to-week ratio showed no relationship.
- Sample: n = 5,205, 1,820 injured, 588,071 sessions, 18-month cohort, 22% female, mean age 45.8, injuries self-reported.
- Source: Schuster Brandt Frandsen et al., "How much running is too much? Identifying high-risk running sessions..." (title truncated in my retrieval), https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/how-much-running-is-too-much-identifying-high-risk-running-sessio/ ; doi:10.1136/bjsports-2024-109380
- Publisher: Br J Sports Med 59(17):1203-1210. pub_date: online 7 July 2025, issue 26 August 2025
- Confidence: medium (large, device-measured, but observational, so not proof that a cap prevents injury)
- Class: empirical. Read depth: abstract (repository record)
- My inference, not the authors': the risk is not monotonic across the first two categories, and 10% is the edge of the chosen reference category. It should not be read as a tested physiological threshold.
- Critique exists but I saw it only as a snippet: a blog (runningwithrock.com, not peer-reviewed) argues the method may attach injuries to shorter runs. Unassessed.

**1.4 A systematic review found very limited evidence that sudden load changes increase injury in runners.** Four studies included, three reporting an association with load increases. No difference was found between 10% and 24% increases. The conclusion gives no progression threshold.
- Source: Damsted et al., "Is there evidence for an association between changes in training load and running-related injuries? A systematic review", https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/is-there-evidence-for-an-association-between-changes-in-training-/
- Publisher: Int J Sports Phys Ther 13(6):931-942. pub_date: December 2018
- Confidence: medium (predates Frandsen 2025, so not current for single-session work)
- Class: empirical (systematic review). Read depth: abstract

**1.5 Earlier, wider reviews also failed to identify specific training errors.** One concluded that it was not possible to identify which training errors relate to injury. A review of prospective studies called the evidence on distance, duration, frequency, intensity and recent changes conflicting.
- Sources: "Training errors and running related injuries: a systematic review", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22389869/ (2012 per search summary); review of prospective studies, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4842282 (attribution to this exact URL not certain; title and date not confirmed)
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (reviews). Read depth: snippet

**1.6 Origin of the 10% rule: not established in this run.** Buist calls it the "10% training rule", but I retrieved no source identifying who first proposed it or on what data. That it began as coaching convention is an **unverified belief**. Class: convention (presumed).

## Q2. Distance or time as the unit

**2.1 No evidence retrieved directly compares weekly distance with weekly time as the better unit for injury or performance.** Search-level findings use different units and point in different directions: over 30 km/week HR 3.28 (1.23-8.75) in one study; more training hours per week associated with fewer injuries (RR 0.575, 0.451-0.731) in another. These come from a table in a review, with underlying studies, populations and confounding not checked.
- Source: Journal of Athletic Training review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9528699 (vol 57(7) per URL; year not confirmed)
- Confidence: low. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet
- Finding: this is absence of head-to-head evidence. The key studies above (1.2, 1.3) measured distance, so the supported thresholds are distance-based. Applying them to time is an untested extrapolation.

## Q3. Acute:chronic workload ratio

**3.1 A reanalysis found ACWR's effect is reproduced with fixed and random denominators, and the authors recommend dismissing it.** Original OR 2.45 (1.28-4.71); fixed chronic load OR 1.95 (1.18-3.52); random chronic loads OR 1.16-2.07. Neither ACWR nor acute load alone beat an intercept-only model meaningfully (c-statistic 0.574 and 0.544 vs 0.5). The data are from players, not runners.
- Source: Impellizzeri et al., "What Role Do Chronic Workloads Play in the Acute to Chronic Workload Ratio? Time to Dismiss ACWR and Its Underlying Theory", https://www.springermedizin.de/what-role-do-chronic-workloads-play-in-the-acute-to-chronic-work/18697072 ; doi:10.1007/s40279-020-01378-6
- Publisher: Sports Medicine. pub_date: online 17 December 2020 (2021 issue)
- Confidence: medium-high on the statistical critique; indirect for runners
- Class: empirical (reanalysis). Read depth: abstract

**3.2 In runners, ACWR did not behave as the traditional model predicts:** negative dose-response in Garmin-RUNSAFE (see 1.3). Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: abstract.

**3.3 The field remains contested: an editorial still argues against abandoning ACWR.** It reports the traditional 0.8-1.3 "sweet spot" and at least 1.5 as high risk, describes the literature as controversial, and cites a study that found no support for the sweet spot.
- Source: Zouhal et al., "Editorial: Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio: Is There Scientific Evidence?", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/
- Publisher: Frontiers in Physiology 12:669687. pub_date: 7 May 2021
- Confidence: medium as a statement of the debate; opinion, not new evidence; mainly team-sport context
- Class: convention/opinion. Read depth: full text (editorial)

Gap: I did not retrieve replies from ACWR proponents.

## Q4. Hard-easy sequencing and intensity distribution

**4.1 One study found 5 km performance was slower after 24 h passive recovery, and on average maintained after 72 h.** R24 slower than baseline, p = .03. Individual variation noted. This measures performance, not injury. Sample and population not seen.
- Source: "Comparison of 5km running performance after 24 and 72 hours of passive recovery", https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4019138?lng=en (database record; journal and date not confirmed)
- Confidence: low. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**4.2 The "48-72 h between hard sessions" and "no two hard days in a row" guidance found in this run came from coaching and blog sources, without controlled evidence.** Sources seen: 220 Triathlon, TrainingPeaks, Quick and Dirty Tips, ctyeh.com (last of these uncited, red flag).
- URL example: https://www.220triathlon.com/training/run-training/how-long-should-you-rest-after-a-big-training-run
- Confidence: low. Class: convention. Read depth: snippet
- Finding: I found no trial testing consecutive hard days vs spaced hard days in recreational runners. One search does not rule out that one exists.

**4.3 Polarised training's advantage is clearest for VO2peak, not other endurance measures.** No evidence of superiority for the other endurance surrogates, and the advantage was mostly seen in interventions under 12 weeks. All competitive levels.
- Source: Oliveira, Boppre and Fonseca, Sports Medicine, https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z.pdf
- pub_date: 2024. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Read depth: snippet

**4.4 In amateur runners, polarised training mostly did not change race time.** Four of five studies found no between-group differences over 2, 5 and 10 km.
- Source: Cruz-González and Arboleda-Serna, scoping review, https://doaj.org/article/57a48291f6ea493dbc800e340399088f
- Publisher: Revista de Investigación e Innovación en Ciencias de la Salud. pub_date: November 2022
- Confidence: low-medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**4.5 Contrary points exist.** A trial of 30 recreational runners reported polarised training can stimulate greater effects than between-thresholds training (Muñoz et al., 2014 per search summary, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23752040/). A 16-study review of middle- and long-distance runners favoured pyramidal and polarised over threshold training (2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29182410/). Confidence: low-medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet.

**4.6 An exact 80/20 split as a default was not validated by any source retrieved.** Treat it as convention. I did not find its origin or a recreational-runner trial of that exact ratio.

## Q5. Long run and missed sessions

**5.1 Ranking the long run first when sessions are missed is coaching convention.** RunnersConnect ranks long runs first, then quality, then easy runs. These are commercial coaching sources. No study compared which session type to protect.
- Source: https://runnersconnect.net/training-flexibility/
- Confidence: high that it is convention; low that it is true. Class: convention. Read depth: snippet
- Finding: I found no controlled comparison of session types, and no guideline on missed sessions. A single search does not rule them out.

**5.2 A longest run and higher volume are associated with faster finishes, not more injury.** For half-marathon runners: over 32 km/week and a long run over 21 km linked to faster finish. n = 556 half and 441 full marathon. Observational, so this is confounded by fitness and experience, and does not show that the long run should be prioritised.
- Source: "Training for a (half-)marathon: Training volume and longest endurance run related to performance and running injuries" (Fokkema et al.), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7496388/
- Publisher: Scand J Med Sci Sports. pub_date: 2020
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**5.3 Long training disruptions are associated with slower marathons.** Disruptions of at least 7 days: finish-time cost 5% in men and 3.5% in women; 5.4% in faster and 2.6% in slower runners. Applies to marathon, not half-marathon, and to long gaps, not single missed sessions.
- Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.1096124/epub
- Publisher: Frontiers in Sports and Active Living. pub_date: 2022 per DOI, not otherwise confirmed
- Confidence: medium-low. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**5.4 Do not "make up" missed runs:** coaching advice only (same coaching sources). Class: convention. Confidence: low. It is compatible with 1.3 by inference, since a make-up long session could create a spike, but this was not tested.

## Q6. Weekly frequency, injury rates and risk factors

**6.1 Novices have a higher exposure-adjusted injury rate than recreational runners.** 17.8 per 1000 h (95% CI 16.7-19.1) vs 7.7 per 1000 h. Definitions were heterogeneous.
- Source: Videbæk et al., https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25951917/
- Publisher: Sports Medicine. pub_date: 2015
- Confidence: medium-high. Class: empirical (systematic review). Read depth: snippet

**6.2 Proportion-based figures point the other way and are not comparable with 6.1.** 14.9% novices (range 9.4-94.9%), 26.1% recreational, 62.6% competitive. Follow-up and exposure differ. The very wide range makes this a poor default.
- Source: Fredette et al., J Athletic Training, https://nata.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/attr/57/7/article-p650.pdf (year not confirmed)
- Confidence: medium-low. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**6.3 Frequency was not significantly associated with injury in a large novice cohort,** with a non-significant trend for three runs per week being riskier than two. Higher intensity in the previous week was associated with higher risk, and lower volume with higher risk. n = 1,696, 6 weeks.
- Source: "The NLstart2run study: Training-related factors associated with running-related injuries in novice runners", https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/the-nlstart2run-study-training-related-factors-associated-with-ru/
- Publisher and date: not confirmed
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet

**6.4 No evidence-based recommended number of weekly runs was found for novices, 5 km or half-marathon goals.** A U-shaped curve (more injury at once per week and 6-7 per week) is reported in a Nielsen review, which also says frequency's specific role cannot be determined. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3290924 . Confidence: low. Class: empirical. Read depth: snippet. Any planner default of about 3 runs per week should be labelled convention. I retrieved no guideline, and frequency-specific half-marathon data were not seen.

## Q7. Combining strength and running

**7.1 Strength training generally improved running economy, by 2-8% vs controls, in middle- and long-distance runners.** 24 studies, economy measured in 20, results not always consistent. Body composition not negatively affected. The authors recommend 2-3 sessions per week, added to running.
- Source: Blagrove, Howatson and Hayes, "Effects of strength training on the physiological determinants of middle- and long-distance running performance: a systematic review", https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Effects_of_strength_training_on_the_physiological_determinants_of_middle-_and_long-distance_running_performance_a_systematic_review/9619553
- Publisher: Sports Medicine 48(5). pub_date: 2018
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (systematic review). Read depth: snippet. Applicability to novices not checked.

**7.2 Strength training substantially reduced sports injuries across sports.** RR 0.315 (0.207-0.480). Stretching showed no benefit: RR 0.963 (0.846-1.095). 25 RCTs. This is not running-specific, so the size should not be transferred to runners.
- Source: Lauersen, Bertelsen and Andersen, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871
- Publisher: Br J Sports Med 48(11):871-877. pub_date: 2014
- Confidence: medium for sport generally; low for recreational runners. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Read depth: snippet
- Not superseded but extended: a 2018 review by the same group reports RR 0.338 (0.238-0.480) with volume and intensity associated with risk reduction (https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/2579164?show=full; attribution to this record not certain). Read depth: snippet.

**7.3 Same-week scheduling (order, spacing between strength and hard runs) was not addressed by anything retrieved.** Finding: no evidence seen. Any spacing rule is convention until checked.

## What this supports for planner defaults (my inference, not source claims)

- Do not present a 10% weekly cap as evidence-based injury prevention (1.1, 1.2, 1.4).
- Add a check of each session against the longest run in the last 30 days, using distance. Label it as an observational association (1.3).
- Do not use ACWR as a rule (3.1-3.3).
- Label hard-day spacing, 80/20, long-run priority and 3 runs per week as convention (4.2, 4.6, 5.1, 6.4).
- Support strength work for running economy, with injury effect labelled as indirect (7.1, 7.2).

## Leads worth chasing

1. Frandsen 2025 full text: limitations, handling of runners with no recent long run, and how low-distance runners behave. Relevant to novices, whose small base makes a 10% limit very tight.
2. Replies to Frandsen 2025 in BJSM, plus the BJSM blog: https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751
3. Full text of Fokkema 2020, NLstart2run, Videbæk 2015 and Blagrove 2018, to replace snippet-level figures.
4. Damsted's "no difference between 10% and 24%" source study, and the Aarhus commentary on progression between 1% and 10%: https://runsafe.au.dk/display/artikel/limited-evidence-on-changes-in-training-load-and-running-related-injury
5. Trial registrations: polarised vs threshold in recreational runners, NCT07498608; running schedule focus and injury: https://ctv.veeva.com/study/focus-of-a-running-schedule-and-risk-of-running-injuries
6. NURMI study on training habits by distance: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1269374
7. ACWR proponent replies to Impellizzeri.
8. Guidelines to check: ACSM, national athletics federations and health bodies, none retrieved here.

## Looked for and could not find

- The historical origin of the 10% rule.
- Any trial of a 10% cap other than Buist 2008, or any trial of a single-session cap.
- A head-to-head comparison of distance and time as the load unit.
- A trial of consecutive hard running days vs spaced hard days in recreational runners.
- A trial of an exact 80/20 distribution in recreational runners.
- Evidence or a reputable guideline on which session to protect after a missed session.
- A guideline-level recommendation on weekly runs for novices, 5 km or half-marathon goals.
- Evidence on scheduling strength and running in the same week, or running-specific injury effects of strength training.

These absences reflect a limited search budget of one or two queries per topic. They are not proof that no such evidence exists.
