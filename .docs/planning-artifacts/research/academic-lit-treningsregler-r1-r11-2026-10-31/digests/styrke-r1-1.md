# Digest: strength training frequency, recovery, splits, concurrent training (styrke-r1-1)

Accessed for all sources: 2026-10-31. Decision served: defensible default values for a rule-based strength planner for healthy recreational adults.

## How to read the evidence labels

Pages were read through a fetch tool that returns a model-extracted answer, and searches returned model-written summaries with excerpts. Neither is the same as reading the raw paper.

- **full text (extracted)**: the full article was fetched and queried through the extraction tool.
- **abstract**: a PubMed abstract was fetched and queried through the extraction tool.
- **press release**: the publisher's or journal's release was fetched, not the paper.
- **search excerpt**: seen only in search results. The source itself was never opened, so verify before relying on it.

Exact figures from "full text (extracted)" should still be spot-checked before they are cited in the product.

All journal articles below are peer-reviewed journal publications, based on the journal named. No preprint is relied on for a claim.

## Q1. Guidelines: sessions per week

**Claim 1.1.** The ACSM 2026 position stand message, as given in the release, is to train all major muscle groups at least twice a week. The release presents this as more important than following a complex or "perfect" plan. It gives no separate advice for beginners and says athletes and highly trained people need more specialised programs. It says nothing on rest or splits.
- Source: News release on "Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews", https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943
- Publisher: EurekAlert (release on an ACSM position stand in Med Sci Sports Exerc). pub_date: 2026-03-16
- Confidence: medium (the paper itself was not read). Class: guideline. Depth: press release.

**Claim 1.2.** The ACSM 2026 stand is an overview of 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants, and it updates the 2009 stand. Resistance training beat no exercise on strength, hypertrophy, power, endurance and several physical function outcomes. Few prescription variables affected primary adaptations.
- Source: ACSM Position Stand, https://journals.lww.com/acsm-msse/fulltext/2026/04000/american_college_of_sports_medicine_position.21.aspx and https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/
- Publisher: Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise (ACSM). pub_date: April 2026 issue.
- Confidence: medium. Class: guideline. Depth: search excerpt. The full-text fetch failed with HTTP 402 (paywall). The stand's detailed frequency conclusions were **not seen**.

**Unverified beliefs (from memory, NOT retrieved in this run, do not cite as found):**
- WHO 2020 recommends muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days a week for adults. Both fetches failed (BMJ returned HTTP 403, PMC returned a reCAPTCHA page).
- ACSM 2009 graded frequency by training status, roughly 2-3 days a week for novices and up to 4-5 for advanced trainees. It was not retrieved. The release says only that the 2026 stand is the first update in about 17 years, so check whether this gradation survived.

## Q2. Frequency: systematic reviews

**Claim 2.1.** Hypertrophy: with volume equated, higher and lower frequencies did not differ significantly (25 studies), including in resistance-trained participants and in upper versus lower body. The authors state that frequency "does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated", and that people can choose frequency per muscle group by preference. Non-volume-equated studies favoured higher frequency, but the difference between 1 and 3+ days a week was modest.
- Source: Schoenfeld, Grgic, Krieger. "How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy?", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/
- Publisher: Journal of Sports Sciences 37(11):1286-1295. pub_date: 2019 (Epub 2018-12-17)
- Confidence: high for the finding as stated in the abstract. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: abstract.
- Note: frequency here is **per muscle group**, not sessions per week. The two are not the same in a planner, because a split can have many sessions and low per-muscle frequency.

**Claim 2.2 (superseded).** The earlier Schoenfeld 2016 meta-analysis (10 studies) concluded that twice a week beat once a week for hypertrophy on a volume-equated basis, and left open whether three times beats twice. The 2019 review, with 25 studies and the same lead author, did not find this difference on a volume-equated basis. The "twice per muscle is superior" claim should not be cited to the 2016 paper alone.
- Source: "Effects of Resistance Training Frequency on Measures of Muscle Hypertrophy", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27102172/
- Publisher: Sports Medicine. pub_date: 2016
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 2.3.** Strength: the Grgic meta-analysis found an overall effect of frequency on strength gains (p = 0.003), but none in the volume-equated subgroup (p = 0.421). The authors conclude that higher frequency can be a way to increase weekly volume and so promote strength gains.
- Source: "Effect of Resistance Training Frequency on Gains in Muscular Strength", https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x
- Publisher: Sports Medicine. pub_date: 2018
- Confidence: medium. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: search excerpt (the fetch was redirected to a login step and not followed).

**Claim 2.4 (contradiction).** Ralston and colleagues concluded that weekly frequency had no significant effect on strength, whether or not volume was equated. This conflicts with the unadjusted result in 2.3. Both agree that there is no frequency effect once volume is equated.
- Source: "Weekly Training Frequency Effects on Strength Gain: A Meta-Analysis", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6081873/
- Publisher: Sports Medicine - Open. pub_date: 2018
- Confidence: low to medium. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: search excerpt.

**Finding 2.5 (absence): more than 5 sessions a week.** No retrieved source tests or supports more than 5 strength sessions a week for recreational adults. The only high-frequency category seen is the open-ended "3+ days a week" in 2.1, which does not separate higher frequencies. Reasonable inference, not a sourced finding: if frequency adds little beyond the volume it carries (2.1, 2.3), there is no retrieved evidence that a planner needs more than 5 sessions. This is only absence in a small search, and it is not evidence of harm or of no benefit.

## Q3. Recovery, rest days, consecutive days

**Claim 3.1.** Three consecutive and three non-consecutive training days a week gave similar strength and body composition results in a randomised trial of 21 recreationally trained men (3 non-consecutive n = 11, 3 consecutive n = 10). Leg press and bench press strength rose in both groups.
- Source: "Nonconsecutive versus consecutive-day resistance training in recreationally trained subjects", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27682004
- Publisher: journal not seen in the retrieved text. pub_date: 2016 per the search summary.
- Confidence: low to medium (small, men only). Class: empirical (RCT). Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 3.2.** A 12-week study of consecutive versus non-consecutive days found no training-by-group interaction for any strength or body composition measure. Strength increased in both groups.
- Source: "Effects of Consecutive Versus Non-consecutive Days of Resistance Training on Strength, Body Composition, and Red Blood Cells", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29967584/ (PMC6015912)
- Publisher and pub_date: not seen in the retrieved text.
- Confidence: low to medium. Class: empirical. Depth: search excerpt (the full-text redirect was not followed, because the call budget was used up).

**Claim 3.3.** The advice to place sessions at least 48 hours apart exists, and studies of consecutive-day training are described as scarce even though many people train that way. These excerpts appeared in the consecutive-day search results, seemingly from the paper in 3.2, but the attribution was not confirmed.
- Source: as in 3.2, attribution unconfirmed.
- Confidence: low. Class: empirical (paper's own commentary). Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 3.4.** Recovery depends on how hard and how much was trained, so 48 hours is not a fixed value. In trained men, training to failure prolonged neuromuscular recovery, with markers still affected at 24 to 48 hours.
- Source: "Time course of recovery following resistance training leading or not to failure", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/
- Publisher: not confirmed (Springer Medicine listing, "Original Article"). pub_date: December 2017
- Confidence: medium for direction, low for generalising. Class: empirical. Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 3.5.** A study of ten loading and set configurations reported that protocols to failure, especially with many repetitions, reduced mechanical function for up to 48 hours. Lower-volume protocols recovered faster between 24 and 48 hours than the highest-volume one. The search summary attributes the two details to two different papers (2017 and 2020), and that split was not confirmed.
- Source: "Time Course of Recovery From Resistance Exercise With Different Set Configurations", https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Time%20Course%20of%20Recovery%20From%20Resistance%20Exercise%20With%20Different%20Set%20Configurations.pdf
- Publisher: not confirmed; hosted on a personal site. pub_date: 2020 per the search summary.
- Confidence: low. Class: empirical. Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 3.6.** Older adults: strength exercise may cause muscle damage taking 24 to 216 hours to recover, which is outside this planner's main group but relevant if older users are included.
- Source: "Recovery from Resistance Exercise in Older Adults: A Systematic Scoping Review", https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40798-023-00597-1/tables/6
- Publisher: Sports Medicine - Open. pub_date: not seen (DOI suggests 2023).
- Confidence: low to medium. Class: empirical (scoping review). Depth: search excerpt.

**Finding 3.7 (absence): "never more than 3 days in a row".** No retrieved source supports, tests or even states this rule, and no source gave a minimum number of rest days per week. The consecutive-day trials (3.1, 3.2) tested exactly 3 consecutive days and found no strength or body composition penalty, so they say nothing about 4 or more. They were not designed around injury or recovery outcomes, per the search summary.

Status: treat the rule as **convention or a product safety choice, not an evidence-based limit**. Only one query targeted this, so the absence is weak.

**Finding 3.8 (absence).** No retrieved source tested whether the same muscle group needs 48 hours in order to gain more. The evidence seen concerns acute recovery markers, not long-term adaptation.

## Q4. Full body versus split

**Claim 4.1.** With volume equated, split and full-body routines gave similar results (14 studies, 392 healthy adults). Bench press MD 1.19 [-1.28, 3.65], p = 0.34. Lower-limb strength MD 2.47 [-2.11, 7.05], p = 0.29. Elbow extensor, elbow flexor and vastus lateralis cross-sectional area and lean mass were all non-significant. The authors suggest choosing by preference.
- Source: Ramos-Campo et al. "Efficacy of Split Versus Full-Body Resistance Training on Strength and Muscle Growth", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/
- Publisher: Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research 38(7):1330-1340. pub_date: 2024-07-01 (Epub 2024-04-09)
- Confidence: medium to high for the comparison. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: abstract.
- Limits, from search excerpts only: mostly male samples and 4 to 12 week study durations. The abstract does not state units for MD.

**Finding 4.2 (absence and inference).** No retrieved source justifies assigning templates by sessions per week as an adaptation-based rule. No source compared upper/lower with push/pull/legs specifically. Inference, not a sourced finding: choosing templates by sessions per week can be defended as a **practical way to distribute volume**, but not as something proven to improve results. Together, 2.1 and 4.1 suggest that volume and preference matter more than the template. The per-muscle "at least twice a week" in 1.1 is a usable constraint on templates, so a once-a-week body-part split would conflict with it.

## Q5. Concurrent strength and endurance

**Claim 5.1.** Concurrent training did not compromise maximal strength (SMD -0.06, 95% CI -0.20 to 0.09, p = 0.446, 37 studies) or hypertrophy (reported as SMD -0.01, CI -0.16 to 0.18, p = 0.919, 15 studies) compared with strength training alone. It did reduce explosive strength gains (SMD -0.28, CI -0.48 to -0.08, p = 0.007, 18 studies).
- Source: Schumann et al. "Compatibility of Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training for Skeletal Muscle Size and Function", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/
- Publisher: Sports Medicine 52(3):601-612. pub_date: 2022 (online 2021-11-10)
- Confidence: high for no effect on maximal strength; medium for explosive strength, which is sensitive to one influential study. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: full text (extracted), about 94% of the page.
- Check: the hypertrophy point estimate looks inconsistent with its CI, whose midpoint is about +0.01. Check the sign against the paper before quoting.
- Limits: mean PEDro 4.3 and no formal certainty grading (GRADE), as reported by the extraction.

**Claim 5.2.** Spacing and order: explosive strength was reduced in same-session training (20 minutes or less between modes; SMD -0.31, CI -0.62 to -0.01, p = 0.043) but not significantly when sessions were at least 3 hours apart. No order-specific effect was found. Aerobic modality, frequency (more versus fewer than 5 weekly sessions), training status and age (under versus over 40) did not significantly moderate the results.
- Source: as 5.1. Confidence: medium. Class: empirical. Depth: full text (extracted).
- Note: these moderator results apply to the explosive-strength analysis as far as the extraction shows. The authors advise separating modes only for people prioritising explosive strength. The "5 weekly sessions" figure refers to concurrent training frequency and does not answer Q2.

**Claim 5.3 (superseded in part).** Wilson 2012 (21 studies, 422 effect sizes) reported hypertrophy effect sizes of 1.23 for strength, 0.27 for endurance and 0.85 for concurrent training. Running, but not cycling, gave significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Schumann 2022 is an updated synthesis that found no such modality difference, so Wilson's result should be treated as older and tentative. It should not drive a "prefer cycling" default.
- Source: "Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises", https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/
- Publisher: Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research 26(8):2293-2307. pub_date: 2012
- Confidence: medium that this is what it reported. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: search excerpt.

**Claim 5.4 (nuance).** Lundberg 2022 found a small negative effect on muscle fibre hypertrophy, which in subgroups appeared for type I fibres with running but not cycling. So fibre-level findings do not fully match the whole-muscle result in 5.1.
- Source: "The Effects of Concurrent Aerobic and Strength Training on Muscle Fiber Hypertrophy", https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354/
- Publisher: not seen in the retrieved text (a search summary calls it a 2022 meta-analysis). pub_date: 2022
- Confidence: low to medium. Class: empirical (meta-analysis). Depth: search excerpt.

## Implications for defaults (my inference from the above, not sourced recommendations)

| Rule | Status |
|---|---|
| At least 2 sessions a week, each major muscle group at least twice a week | Supported by ACSM 2026 (release only) |
| Upper cap of 5 sessions a week | Product choice. No evidence of benefit above it was found, and no evidence of harm |
| 48 hours between same-muscle sessions | Soft default, longer after failure or high volume. Not shown to be a requirement for gains |
| Maximum 3 days in a row | Convention or safety choice. Label it that way |
| Template by sessions per week | Practical choice. Outcomes are similar when volume is equal |
| Strength and endurance spacing | Only needs enforcing for explosive-strength goals (3 hours or more). No order rule is justified |
