# Research prompt: rules for safe, progressive training plans (running + strength)

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research tool (ChatGPT Deep Research, Gemini Deep Research, Perplexity, etc.).

---

## Context and decision

I am a student team building "Flex PT", a web-based AI personal trainer for recreational adults (strength training and running). Training plans are NOT freely generated by a language model. They are generated by a predefined, deterministic rule set in code, and every plan change proposed by the AI coach is validated against that rule set before it is applied. I need to decide what that rule set contains.

The rule set must be safe, evidence-based and simple enough to implement and test. Target users are healthy adults (not athletes, not people needing medical follow-up) with busy, shifting schedules who often have to cut a planned week from e.g. four sessions down to two.

I will use your findings to (1) write concrete product requirements and (2) define an "expected plan" for four test profiles:

1. Beginner: run a first 5 km (running)
2. Complete a half marathon (running)
3. "Get started on my fitness journey" (general beginner strength)
4. Achieve a first pull-up (strength)

## Research questions (in priority order)

Answer each as explicit, implementable rules or ranges, with the strength of evidence stated.

### A. Running
1. **Beginner 5 km (couch-to-5K type):** typical structure, number of sessions per week, run/walk interval progression, plan length in weeks, and what consensus programs use (e.g. NHS Couch to 5K, Parkrun, Runner's World, ACSM). Where do they agree and differ?
2. **Half marathon:** typical plan lengths and weekly volume ranges for beginner and intermediate runners, long-run progression, taper length, and the prerequisite base fitness before starting.
3. **Weekly load progression:** what does the evidence say about the "10% rule" and other limits on weekly distance or time increases? Include injury-risk studies, and note where the rule is criticized or unsupported. Include recommendations on deload or recovery weeks (e.g. every 3rd or 4th week).
4. **Rest and intensity distribution:** minimum rest days, spacing of hard sessions, the share of easy versus hard running (e.g. 80/20), and long-run limits as a share of weekly volume.

### B. Strength training
5. **General beginner strength:** recommended weekly frequency, full-body versus split structure for beginners, sets, reps and rest intervals per exercise, and a sensible standard exercise set without specialised equipment beyond a typical gym. Cover guidance from ACSM, WHO and NSCA.
6. **Progression rules:** how to progress load or reps (linear progression, double progression, RPE/RIR-based), how much increase per step or week is typical, and when to hold, repeat or reduce load. Cover deload rules and how to handle missed sessions.
7. **First pull-up:** progression pathway (e.g. dead hangs, scapular pulls, negatives, band-assisted, inverted rows), typical frequency, volume and timelines, and prerequisite strength.
8. **Minimum effective dose:** what is the minimum weekly volume or frequency that still gives meaningful progress (e.g. 2 sessions per week)? This matters because users often fall short of the plan.

### C. Combining running and strength
9. How should the two be scheduled together in one week (order within a day, spacing, session caps, rest day rules)? What does the evidence on the "interference effect" say for recreational athletes?

### D. Adapting when life gets in the way
10. **Missed sessions and compressed weeks:** what do coaches and the literature recommend when a user misses a session, misses a week, or can only train 2 of 4 days? Should missed sessions be rescheduled, skipped or merged (e.g. two full-body sessions instead of a split)? How should load be reduced after 1, 2, or 3+ weeks off (detraining and return-to-training guidance)?
11. **Low energy, fatigue and soreness:** simple, safe adjustment rules based on self-reported energy (e.g. RPE or a readiness score).

### E. Safety limits for an automated, non-medical product
12. Which hard guard-rails should an automated plan generator never cross for a general adult population (e.g. maximum sessions per week, minimum rest, maximum weekly increase)? Which user conditions or red flags (pain, injury, pregnancy, medical conditions, age) should trigger "consult a professional" instead of an automated plan? Cite pre-participation screening guidance (e.g. PAR-Q+, ACSM).

## Output format (important)

- Start with a short summary of the most important findings and any decisions I should make.
- Then give findings per research question (A1–E12), in this order. For each one, state:
  - the **recommended rule or range** in a form that could be implemented in code (numbers, conditions),
  - the **evidence strength** (strong, moderate, weak/expert opinion),
  - where sources **disagree**.
- End with a **proposed rule-set table** (rule, value or range, applies to running, strength or both, source).
- End with a sample week-by-week outline for each of the four test profiles above, labelled clearly as an illustration.
- Include a **glossary** of the terms used (e.g. RPE, RIR, deload, taper, progressive overload).
- End with a complete **source list**.

## Source and evidence requirements (non-negotiable)

- **Every claim needs a source with URL and publication date.** If you cannot source a claim, say so explicitly; do not fill gaps with plausible-sounding guidance.
- Prefer primary and authoritative sources: position stands and guidelines (ACSM, WHO, NSCA, NHS, UK Chief Medical Officers), systematic reviews and meta-analyses, peer-reviewed studies, and established coach-authored programs. Use blogs, forums and commercial fitness sites only as labelled secondary sources.
- **Two independent sources are required** for every safety limit and every numeric progression limit (e.g. the 10% rule, minimum rest days, maximum weekly increase).
- Prefer sources from the last 10 years for guidelines. Mark any source older than that and explain why it is still used.
- Report **contrary evidence** and disputed rules (e.g. weak support for the 10% rule) rather than presenting a single consensus.
- Say clearly where the evidence is thin or based on expert opinion only.
- State your date of research, and note any claim that may be outdated.
- Do not give medical advice. Frame everything as input for the design of a general-population, non-medical product.
